What each company changed along the way

  • 2016: Instagram replaced its chronological feed with one ranked by predicted interest (TechCrunch). YouTube publishes its deep-learning recommender paper.
  • 2017: Reddit launches r/popular, a feed of posts from communities you don't follow (TechCrunch).
  • Jan 2018: YouTube: more than 70% of watch time comes from its recommendations (CNET).
  • Aug 2020 / Mar 2021: Instagram Reels and YouTube Shorts launch in the U.S., both answers to TikTok.
  • 2022–23: Meta says AI-recommended content is about 40% of what people see on Instagram (Q1 2023 earnings call). Reddit's home feed becomes machine-learned and mixes in communities you don't follow.

Why this is good business

  • Time is inventory. These apps make money from ads, and every extra minute is more ad slots. Ranking systems are trained to predict what keeps you watching, liking and sharing, because that's what fills those slots.
  • There's no bottom. The engineer who designed infinite scroll has said he regrets it, comparing endless feeds to "behavioural cocaine" (BBC, 2018). With no natural stopping point, the session ends when you decide, not when the content does.
  • Unpredictable rewards. Most videos are fine; every so often one is great. That variable payoff keeps you swiping for the next good one, the same pattern slot machines rely on.
  • Short clips mean more guesses. A 20-second video gives the model a fresh prediction to make (and a fresh signal to learn from) every few seconds, so it learns what hooks me far faster than an hour-long video ever could.
  • Strangers are an endless supply. A feed limited to people I follow runs out. A feed of recommended strangers never does, which is exactly the shift in section 02.

07 · How the machine sees me

My likes are training data

Every like, upvote and skip on this page was also a signal. None of these apps has one "algorithm". Each runs separate ranking systems for its feeds (Instagram's Feed, Stories, Explore and Reels; YouTube's Home, Up Next and Shorts; Reddit's Home and Popular). They work the same basic way: gather a few thousand possible posts, predict what I'll do with each one, and put the best predictions at the top.

The feedback loop

My data fits this loop, but it can't prove it: I can't separate my taste changing from the app steering it. Both are probably true.

What does "pet video" actually mean to a machine?

The categories on this page are mine. I named the topics, and a small language model (Instagram) or I myself (Reddit) decided which one each post belonged to. The platforms mostly don't work from a list of named categories like that. They turn every post, and every person, into a long list of numbers (an embedding). Posts that people engage with in similar ways end up with similar numbers, close together.

So when an app "learns I like pet videos," there is usually no switch labelled pets. Its guess about me moves toward a region full of animal clips. Named topics do exist where people need to see them, like the ad topics I can ask to see less of, or Google Discover's "less like Cryptocurrency" button. The ranking itself runs on the numbers.

Beyond likes: the other signals in my downloads

Likes are only one input. The rest of the exports fill in most of the picture, and show where it stops.

  • Shares. Instagram names resharing as one of its most important predictions. I went from sending about 4 reels for every 100 I liked in 2022 to about 11 in 2025. Friends' posts are about 8% of what I send but only 3% of what I like: sharing is the more social signal.
  • Everything I scrolled past. Instagram's download covers only two weeks of what I viewed; I liked about 1 in 3. YouTube's keeps a few months of watch history, and most of those videos came from channels I don't subscribe to. The systems learned from everything I didn't like too.
  • Telling it "no". 407 "Not interested" taps on Instagram and on YouTube, against tens of thousands of likes. The most common topic I rejected on Instagram was fitness, which is also about 10% of what I like: to the system, the fitness posts I like and the ones I reject sit in different neighborhoods.
  • Advertising. Instagram's download lists 5,311 advertisers that uploaded a customer list matched to my profile. The ranking and ad systems learn from the same behavior.
  • Still missing: watch time. The signal Reels and Shorts weigh most is whether I watch to the end or swipe away within seconds. No file in any of my downloads records how long I watched anything.

Data. Instagram "Download your information" (HTML), Reddit data request (CSV) and Google Takeout (YouTube activity). YouTube's export keeps likes, dislikes, subscriptions and "Not interested" taps back to 2012 but only the last few months of watch history. Times are US Central.

Method. Reddit votes aren't dated, so each is dated by interpolating Reddit's sequential IDs between my own dated posts. Instagram categories come from all-MiniLM-L6-v2 embeddings, sentiment from twitter-roberta-base-sentiment-latest. Sessions are runs of likes under 10 minutes apart.

Privacy. Only aggregates are published: counts and shares by year, hour and category. No account, channel or subreddit names, captions, titles, searches, messages or individual timestamps.
