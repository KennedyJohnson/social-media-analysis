A personal data story

Eleven years of double-taps

I downloaded my Instagram data and turned every post I've ever liked into data. No names, no captions, just broad patterns: what I'm into, when I scroll, and how much of it the algorithm chose for me.

The biggest shifts

The categories that changed the most, share of likes per year since 2019. Each panel has its own scale.

My feed went from friends to animals. In 2019, posts from friends and life updates were 17% of what I liked; since 2023 they're about 3%. Pets & Animals went the other way, from 2% in 2021 to roughly one in five likes today, the biggest category. Tech & AI is the quiet riser, tripling from 1.5% to over 4%. Sports had one big year: 12% of my likes in 2022, then back to about 3%.

I scroll all day, not just at night. My likes split almost evenly between morning, afternoon and evening, about 30% each, and only about 1 in 7 comes late at night.

03 · Sessions

Scrolling sessions

A session is a run of likes with less than 10 minutes between them. It's a proxy, since scrolling without liking is invisible, but it shows the shape of my habit.

How long sessions run

Number of sessions by likes per session, since 2023

Most sessions are short, but most likes come from long ones. A typical session is 4 likes over about 2 minutes, yet 60% of all my likes came in sessions of 20 or more. In 2026 the typical session doubled to 8 likes.

04 · The comment section

Reading the comments

I've also liked tens of thousands of comments. The export doesn't include the comment text, only which post it was on and when, so these are matched back to the posts I liked to see where I go digging.

Comment likes per post like

By quarter, since 2022

I read the comments more than ever. In early 2022 I liked about 1 comment for every 5 posts; by 2026 it's nearly 1 to 1. Four in five comment likes happen in the same minute I like the post, so it's part of one quick read rather than a later return.

The mood of my feed

Captions were scored with a RoBERTa model trained on tweets (cardiffnlp/twitter-roberta-base-sentiment-latest), which handles emoji and slang better than word-list methods. Score = P(positive) − P(negative), from −1 to +1.

Since 2022. Thin line: each month. Bold lines: yearly average. All values are positive (0 is neutral), so this is zoomed in to show the trend.

Most and least positive topics

Average caption sentiment, the top three and bottom three categories

My feed is positive, but less than it used to be. Average caption sentiment fell every year, from +0.38 in 2022 to +0.27 in 2026, and the share of negative captions rose from 8% to 13%. Friends' posts are the most positive category (+0.60); news and politics the least (+0.20).

06 · The algorithm

Who chose what I saw?

If my likes went to accounts I follow, I was choosing. If they spread across thousands of strangers, the recommendation feed was. Each panel tracks one signal per year.

How the feed took over, and my likes with it

My likes per year compared with before Reels (2019–21 = 1×), with the changes Instagram made along the way

  • 2016: Instagram replaced its chronological feed with one ordered by what it predicts you'll care about (TechCrunch).
  • Aug 2020: Reels launched in the U.S. (Instagram).
  • Jul 2022: Mark Zuckerberg: "about 15% of content in a person's Facebook feed and a little more than that of their Instagram feed is recommended by our AI… We expect these numbers to more than double by the end of next year" (Q2 2022 earnings call).
  • Apr 2023: "Across all of Instagram, that's about 40% of the content that you see" (Q1 2023 earnings call).

The algorithm took over. Instagram launched Reels in the U.S. in August 2020, its answer to TikTok. You can watch it take over my likes: 1% Reels in 2020, 8% in 2021, 54% in 2022 and 87% by 2023. In 2019, my top 10 accounts got half of my likes. In 2026, 94% are Reels and my top 10 get just 3%: my likes are spread across a huge number of accounts I don't know. Only about 3% of this year's likes went to accounts I follow. (Follow data only includes accounts I follow now, so earlier years are approximate.) Discovery tells the same story: in 2019 about 1 in 5 of my likes went to an account I'd never liked before; since 2022 it's been between half and two thirds. One surprise: I expected hashtags to fade as the algorithm took over, but the share of liked posts using them rose from 41% to 63%. Creators still tag heavily, even though ranking now leans on what the video contains and how people react to it.

07 · How the machine sees me

My likes are training data

Every like on this page was also a signal to Instagram. The app doesn't have one "algorithm". It runs separate ranking systems for Feed, Stories, Explore and Reels, and each one predicts what I'll do next and orders content by those predictions. Here is how my own data lines up with what Instagram says those systems look at.

The feedback loop

My data fits this loop, but it can't prove it: I can't separate my taste changing from the app steering it. Both are probably true.

What does "pet video" actually mean to a machine?

The categories on this page are mine. I named 20 topics, and a small language model decided which one each post's caption and hashtags sounded most like. Instagram mostly doesn't work from a list of named categories like that. It turns every post, and every person, into a long list of numbers (an embedding). Posts that people tend to engage with in the same way end up with similar numbers, close together.

So when Instagram "learns I like pet videos," there is usually no switch labelled pets. Its guess about me moves toward a region full of animal clips. Because sound is part of the embedding, a cat video with a trending song can sit nearer to other videos using that song than to a quiet cat photo. Named topics do exist where people need to see them: my download includes a list of ad topics I asked to see less of. The ranking itself runs on the numbers.

Sources: Meta AI, Learning from videos to understand the world (March 2021, on Reels video embeddings); Engineering at Meta, Scaling the Instagram Explore recommendations system (August 2023, on two-tower retrieval).

Beyond likes: the other signals in my download

Likes are only one input. The rest of the export fills in most of the picture, and shows where it stops.

  • Shares. Instagram names resharing as one of its most important predictions. My DMs show I went from sending about 4 reels for every 100 I liked in 2022 to about 11 in 2025. Almost everything I shared (89%) I had also liked. But sharing isn't just liking more: friends' posts are about 8% of what I send but only 3% of what I like, and news is 4% versus 2%. Sharing is the more social signal.
  • Everything I scrolled past. The download covers only two weeks of what I watched and viewed. Of the reels and posts it lists, I liked about 1 in 3, so the system learned from the other two thirds too.
  • Telling it "no". I've tapped "Not interested" 407 times on Explore and Reels. The most common topic I rejected was fitness, which is also about 10% of what I like. That's the "pet video" point in practice: to the system, the fitness posts I like and the ones I reject sit in different neighborhoods, even though my categories lump them together.
  • Advertising. The download lists 5,311 advertisers that "uploaded an audience list containing entries that were matched to" my profile. These are typically customer lists (emails, phone numbers) that businesses upload and Meta matches to accounts. The ranking systems and the ad systems learn from the same behavior.
  • Still missing: watch time. This is the signal Instagram weighs most for Reels. Its predictions are about whether I'll watch to the end, give up within 3 seconds, or watch longer than 95% of viewers. No file in the download records how long I watched anything.

Sources: Adam Mosseri, Instagram Ranking Explained (Instagram, May 2023); Meta Transparency Center, Instagram Reels system card and how Meta explains ranking; Meta, How AI influences what you see on Facebook and Instagram (June 2023).

Method. Parsed from Instagram's "Download your information" HTML export with Python. Categories come from all-MiniLM-L6-v2 embeddings matched to hand-written category descriptions (posts with too little text are marked Unclear and left out of the shares). Sentiment is twitter-roberta-base-sentiment-latest. Instagram exports times at a fixed UTC−8 offset; they are converted to US Central time (with daylight saving).
